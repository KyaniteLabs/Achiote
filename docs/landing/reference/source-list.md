# Source List

Where to look, and how much to trust what you find. Pair this with
`source-quality-ladder.md` (which says how to weigh) and `evidence-ledger.md`
(which says how to record). This file says where to start.

A rule before the list: never fabricate a citation, a price, or a stock level.
If you cannot name a real source, name the kind of source and the search you
would run, and mark the result as inference until it is verified.

## What counts as authoritative in THIS domain

Family food memory is unusual: the most authoritative source is often the least
documented one. A grandmother's hand is a primary source. Weight accordingly.

1. **Primary / oral** - the user's own memory, a living relative, a community
   elder, a neighborhood cook, a community or church cookbook. Highest authority
   for "what this family actually ate," lowest for spelling and pan-regional
   claims. Treat as `user-said` or `researched` depending on who reports it.
2. **Scholarly / reference** - food encyclopedias, food-science texts, peer
   food-studies work. Highest authority for mechanism, history, and regional
   variation. Lower for one family's specific version.
3. **Practitioner** - regional cookbooks by credible authors, diaspora food
   writers, established culinary institutions. Good for technique and variants.
4. **Open web / crowd** - blogs, forums, video, marketplace listings. Useful for
   leads, spellings, and local availability. Never let one carry a strong claim
   alone. Corroborate before promoting from `inferred` to `researched`.

## Anchor references (real, durable)

Confirm editions and details when it matters; do not quote page numbers you have
not seen.

- **Harold McGee, "On Food and Cooking"** - the default for food-science
  mechanism (Maillard, emulsions, starch, volatiles). Use for the sensory "why."
- **Alan Davidson, "The Oxford Companion to Food"** - dish identity, origins,
  regional naming, ingredient history.
- **"Larousse Gastronomique"** - technique and classical preparation reference.
- **"The Flavor Bible" (Page & Dornenburg)** - pairing logic for building a cue
  when the exact item is missing.
- **Gastronomica** and **Food and Foodways** - peer-reviewed food-studies
  journals for diaspora, migration, and authenticity questions.
- **FooDB** and **FlavorDB** - ingredient flavor-compound databases for
  compound-level substitution reasoning. Verify a compound before claiming a
  match; mark unconfirmed overlaps as inference.

## By research task: where to start, and the catch

| Task | Start here | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identify a dish from a sound-alike | Davidson; language dictionaries and Wiktionary for transliteration; regional diaspora food writers | A blog title is a lead, not a confirmation. Spelling drifts across regions. |
| Resolve spelling / dialect | Wiktionary, language-specific dictionaries, the user's family pronunciation | Keep the original sound-alike visible. Do not "correct" away evidence. |
| Regional variants | Reference works + multiple regional cookbooks | "Authentic" usually means "the version near one person." Name the spread, not one winner. |
| Sensory mechanism | McGee; FooDB / FlavorDB | Mechanism explains the cue even when identity is unknown. State it as science, not as dish proof. |
| Local sourcing / substitutes | `local-access-playbook.md`; ethnic-market directories; specialty importers | Never invent live price or stock. If unsearched, label it likely-path guidance. |
| Allergen / safety check | Common-allergen knowledge + the product's own label | You are not a medical or allergy authority. See `rules.md` safety boundary. |

## How to record a source

For every substantial claim, the ledger entry should carry: the claim, the
source type from the ladder, what it does and does not prove, and what would
change your mind. A strong source for one thing (mechanism) can be a weak source
for another (one family's recipe). Weight per claim, not per source.

## When you cannot verify

If web or search tools are unavailable, say so plainly, work from the user's
clues plus food-science mechanism, and keep every unconfirmed claim in
`inferred` or `unknown`. A clearly-bounded "I could not verify this" beats a
confident guess dressed as a fact.
