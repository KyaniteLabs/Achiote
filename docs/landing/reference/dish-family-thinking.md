# Dish-Family Thinking

A fragment rarely points to one dish. It points to a **family**: a cluster of
dishes that share a core and diverge by region and household. Thinking in
families is how you stay honest. It lets you be useful before you know the exact
name, and it stops you from forcing a memory into the first dish you recognize.

This is a way of reasoning, not a lookup table. If a memory does not fit a family
you know, that is a new family to research, not a wrong answer to correct.

## The four moves

1. **Hear the fragment as a name cloud, not a spelling.** A remembered word is one
   point in a cloud of aliases, sound-alikes, transliterations, family nicknames,
   menu names, and typos. "Dol-ma-dees", "holupki", and "sarmale" can all live in
   the same family. Keep the user's original word visible; it is evidence.
2. **Find the family by its shared spine.** Most families share a small set of
   core elements that define them. Match on the spine, not on one ingredient.
3. **Separate shared from divergent.** The shared elements are the family's spine.
   The divergent elements are the axes along which regions and households differ.
4. **Locate the person on the divergent axes.** The nostalgia trigger is almost
   always a *divergent* element, the specific version they grew up with, not the
   shared spine everyone has. That divergent element is what you confirm and what
   the first taste should test.

## Worked example: stuffed vegetables

The name cloud is wide: dolma, dolmades, sarma, tolma, warak enab, yaprak sarma,
golabki, halupki, sarmale, japrak, and the sound-alikes and plurals of each.
Across Arabic, Turkish, Greek, Armenian, Polish, Romanian, and Russian, the word
changes but the family holds.

- **Shared spine:** a vegetable wrapper, a rice or grain filling, herb-forward
  seasoning, slow-cooked in liquid. If those four hold, you are in the family,
  even with no name.
- **Divergent axes:** wrapper (grape leaf vs cabbage vs pepper), meat or no meat,
  sauce (tomato vs egg-and-lemon vs yogurt), served hot or cold.
- **Likely nostalgia trigger:** the specific herb combination, the wrapper
  texture, or the sauce. A grape leaf in egg-and-lemon, served cold, is a very
  different memory from a cabbage roll in tomato, served hot, even though both are
  "stuffed vegetables."

So when someone says "my grandmother made little stuffed leaves in a tangy lemon
sauce, served cold," you do not need the word *dolmades* to help. The spine places
the family; "tangy lemon, cold" places them on the sauce and temperature axes; and
the first cheap taste can test a grape-leaf-and-rice bite with an egg-lemon note
before anyone sources a single specialty item.

## Why this keeps the work investigative

- It lets you be useful with zero name, because the spine is enough to start.
- It stops keyword-matching. You are placing a memory in a family and on its axes,
  not gluing the user's words into a dish.
- It keeps uncertainty honest. "I think this is the stuffed-vegetable family,
  medium confidence; what places you is the sauce" is a researcher's sentence.
  "This is dolmades, here is a recipe" is a summarizer's.
- It connects straight to the cue: test the shared spine cheaply first, then
  confirm the one divergent element that carries the memory.

## How to record it

In the evidence ledger, keep the family and the axes separate from the guess:

- `User said`: the fragment, the sound-alike, the sensory clues.
- `Inferred`: the likely family (from the spine) and the likely position on the
  divergent axes.
- `Unknown`: which exact regional version, pending one more clue.
- `Would change my mind`: a divergent element that does not fit the family at all
  (for example, a sweet filling), which points to a different family.

Never present the family as a closed menu. It is scaffolding for a better
question, not the answer.
