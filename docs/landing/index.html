You might know it by another name.

Achiote is annatto. It's urucum, roucou, atsuete, achiotl, bija, kesumba, beninoki — a single seed called a hundred names across every continent it touched. The name changes. The memory doesn't. That's why this project exists.

Every culture that encountered this seed gave it their own name. Each one tells a story — of trade routes, of colonization, of grandmothers passing down the only word they had for the thing that made the rice red. Food memory works the same way: the name you remember may be wrong, borrowed, or half-forgotten. But the taste underneath is real.

Achiotl
Nahuatl — Mexico

"Shiny seed." The original name. The Aztecs used it to color manuscripts and ritual body paint. Every other name on this list descends from this one word.

Achiote
Spanish — Latin America & Caribbean

The name we chose. Adapted from Taíno via Nahuatl. Spanish colonizers heard "achiotl" and spread it from Mexico to the Caribbean to the Philippines. It is the word most of the world knows today.

Uruku
Tupi — Brazil

"Red." The Tupi people of the Amazon crushed the seeds into a paste for face paint, sun protection, and ceremony. The word became urucum in Portuguese — still the primary name in Brazil.

Bija
Carib — Venezuela & Colombia

The original Carib plant name. Before Spanish or Portuguese arrived, the Carib peoples already had a word for the tree that paints. It survives in Venezuelan Spanish today.

Anato
Portuguese — Portugal & Brazil

From "a-netto" — "the seed." Portuguese traders gave it this utilitarian name. It became the scientific and commercial label that traveled to Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Annatto
English — Global

The international trade name. Derived from Portuguese, adopted by British and Dutch merchants. It's the word on EU food labels, American cheese ingredient lists, and global shipping manifests.

Roucou
French Creole — Trinidad & Tobago

The stew name. In Trinidad, roucou isn't just a spice — it's a water extract cured with salt that gives pelau and stew fish their deep red color. Every grandmother has her own jar.

Atsuete
Tagalog — Philippines

Brought by the Manila galleons. Spanish colonizers introduced achiote to the Philippines in the 16th century. Filipinos adapted the word into atsuete — now essential in kare-kare, adobo, and pancit.

紅木
Japanese — Beninoki

"Red tree." Japanese traders encountered annatto through Dutch merchants in the 17th century. They named it for what it does: turn things red. It became a culinary curiosity in Nagasaki.

Kesumba
Indonesian — Java & Sumatra

The native name that stuck. Unlike the Philippines, Indonesia already had a word for the plant before European contact. Kesumba colors nasi kuning and traditional Javanese stews to this day.

Dầu màu điều
Vietnamese

"Annatto oil." Vietnamese cooks use the oil to color bún bò Huế, batters, and coconut curries. The French brought the seed; the Vietnamese made it indispensable to their cuisine.

लटकन
Hindi — Latkan

"Pendant" or "ornament." In India, the seed was named for how it hangs — like jewelry. It was cultivated commercially by 1787 after British traders recognized its value as a saffron substitute.

The starting point is food science, not a better recipe search.

Fat carries aroma.1
Many aromatic compounds are fat-soluble. A small amount of oil, butter, or rendered fat can release the spice profile you're looking for — though too much fat traps those same aromatics.
Browning creates the compounds that stick.2,7
Roasting, charring, and frying produce hundreds of flavor molecules (pyrazines, thiophenes, furans) that raw ingredients cannot mimic. These are among the strongest olfactory triggers for food memory.
Acid brightens richness.3
Vinegar, citrus, and fermentation provide perceptual contrast to fat — stimulating salivation and activating a different taste pathway. Often the missing "that's it" signal.
Starch is a texture system.4
Rice, bread, potato, masa, yuca, and plantain each create distinct mouthfeels through gelatinization and retrogradation. They carry sauce, not just calories.
Sugar rounds sharpness.5
A small amount of sweetness can balance chile heat, acidity, and bitterness without making the dish taste like dessert.
Contrast matters.6,7
Crisp/soft, hot/cool, creamy/spicy, chewy/tender — texture contrast activates reward pathways and is one of the strongest drivers of how a bite gets remembered.

What is Achiote?

Achiote is a source-available Model Context Protocol (MCP) server that helps AI agents reconstruct nostalgic dishes from fragmented food memories. It provides 15 deterministic tools for memory collection, research planning, provenance tracking, sensory analysis, and generating minimum viable nostalgia cues.

How does food memory reconstruction work?

Achiote follows a 5-step workflow: collect the memory fragment, plan research hypotheses, build an evidence-separated dossier, generate the smallest sensory cue to test, and help source ingredients. Your AI does the live research; Achiote keeps it structured, honest, and organized.

What is a minimum viable nostalgia cue?

The smallest practical aroma, bite, sip, or condiment that tests whether a reconstructed memory is on the right track. It uses grocery-store ingredients and takes under 15 minutes — before anyone commits to a full recipe or shops for specialty items.

What are other names for achiote?

Achiote is known as annatto (English), urucum (Portuguese/Brazil), roucou (French/Caribbean), atsuete (Tagalog/Philippines), bija (Carib/Andes), onoto (Venezuela), kesumba (Indonesian), beninoki (Japanese), latkan (Hindi), and achiotl (Nahuatl) — among dozens of others across every continent.

Can I use Achiote with ChatGPT or Claude?

Yes. Achiote runs as an MCP server. Add it to Claude Code via .mcp.json, to OpenAI Codex via config.toml, or to any agent that supports the Model Context Protocol. Hosted API and MCP production access require a commercial license.
