Most food memories start before the recipe exists.

A person may only remember a smell, a texture, a nickname, a grocery aisle, or the person who made it. Member Berries treats those fragments as evidence to organize, not a recipe to hallucinate.

1

The name may be wrong

Misspellings, phonetic fragments, family nicknames, and borrowed-language names are normal inputs, not failures.

2

The trigger may be tiny

The thing that matters may be one browned edge, one spice bloom, one sour note, one chewy bite, or one table ritual.

3

Evidence stays separated

User memory, researched facts, model inference, and unknowns stay separate so the host can say what it knows and what it is guessing.

Generate the minimum viable cue

Offer the easiest, cheapest, findable sensory test before any full recipe or specialty sourcing.

The useful answer is food science, not a fancier recipe search.

Fat carries aroma.
A tiny amount of oil, butter, dairy, or rendered fat can reveal whether the spice profile is right.
Maillard creates memory.
Browning, charring, and frying produce roasted/meaty notes that raw ingredients cannot mimic.
Acid cuts fat.
Vinegar, citrus, fermentation, and tomato brightness can be the missing “that’s it” signal.
Starch carries sauce.
Rice, bread, potato, masa, yuca, and plantain are texture systems, not just ingredients.
Sugar rounds sharpness.
Sweetness can balance chile, acid, bitterness, and smoke without making the dish “dessert.”
Contrast matters.
Crisp/soft, hot/cool, creamy/spicy, and chewy/tender can carry the nostalgic bite.

Examples that are not solved by a recipe search.

These are intentionally different from the development test cases. The point is the reusable pattern: clues → research → sensory mechanism → minimum viable cue.

Name fragmentA crispy street snack in a paper cone. Chickpea-ish, hot, with green sauce and something dark-sweet.
Workflow: research candidate snack families → isolate fried crunch, herb sauce, sweet-acid contrast → test one bite.
Place + ingredientA soup an aunt made once. Sour, pale, lots of dill, maybe dairy, but no one remembers the name.
Workflow: separate acid source, herb aroma, fat/body, and serving temperature → test a one-cup aroma-sip.
Sensory plateA chewy lunchbox rice cake with sesame-oil smell, browned edges, and a sweet-salty glaze.
Workflow: identify starch gel texture, toasted fat aroma, Maillard edge, and sugar-salt balance → test two bites.

Trust boundaries are part of the UX.

Member Berries should make the host AI more useful by making uncertainty visible and the next test smaller.

  • The host AI performs live web/search research; the MCP server structures the work.
  • Evidence is separated into user-said, researched, inferred, and unknown claims.
  • Minimum viable cues come before full recipes or specialty shopping.
  • No proprietary recipe theft: store facts, citations, and source metadata, not copied recipe prose.
  • Food-memory and dementia-adjacent inspiration are not medical claims.
